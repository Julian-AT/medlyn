\begin{abstract}
We present Medlyn, a prototype that constructs a clinical view for a clinician's role
and task. We extract atomic facts from a longitudinal record, retain their source
locations, and select fact identifiers for registered display templates. We apply a
shared claim validator to cards, chat answers, letters and text prepared for speech.
We distinguish documented negatives, missing documentation and checks that could not
be completed. We also describe a synthetic clinical corpus and an experiment that
adapts a small language model to verify claims. We report regression, synthetic
pipeline, retrieval and verifier evaluations separately. Measurements enter the
manuscript only after human review of their values and source identities; unreviewed
or missing measurements remain pending. We do not establish clinical efficacy or
transfer from generated notes to patient care.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A longitudinal patient record contains more information than a clinician needs for a
single task. The relevant subset changes with the task. Preparing an intervention,
reviewing a tumour-board recommendation and checking follow-up require different
facts from the same documents. An accurate general summary can omit a required
answer. A fluent task-specific summary can also change a recommendation into a
completed procedure or treat missing documentation as a negative finding.

We study the construction of a clinical view with explicit evidence constraints.
Medlyn maps a role and task to information needs, selects stored facts and presents
short English statements linked to German passages. We call this view a \emph{lens}.
We use language models for extraction and selection, but registered templates render
the fact lines. When a model composes a headline or answer, we validate its claims
against the cited facts and original context before release.

Our contribution is a system design that joins task-specific selection to a common
claim boundary. We use the same patient, corpus revision and evidence window across
retrieval, documentation checks, findings and letters. We keep availability,
assertion, temporal status, completeness, conflict and clinician review as separate
state dimensions. We further describe a synthetic data pipeline and a local verifier
experiment. These components provide testable mechanisms; their implementation alone
does not establish their accuracy.

We use transformer language models \citep{attention} for extraction and selection,
with source evidence retained outside the model parameters. We organise the report
around the architecture, data construction and verification experiment.
We exclude challenge passages, patient-level predictions
and private evaluation evidence from this report.

\section{Background}
We distinguish factual support from answer coverage. Factuality studies in
summarisation show why fluent text needs evaluation against its source
\citep{maynez,frank}. In clinical summarisation, both the evaluation metric and the
kind of error matter. \citet{adams} examine faithfulness metrics for hospital-course
summaries, and \citet{famesumm} study medical-summary faithfulness. The reader study
of \citet{vanveen} evaluates adapted models on specified summarisation tasks. We do
not transfer those results to our workflow or use a generic text score as a measure
of clinical utility.

Evidence attribution and atomic decomposition provide relevant design choices.
ALCE evaluates answer quality and citation support \citep{alce}; RARR retrieves
attribution and revises unsupported output \citep{rarr}. FActScore assesses the
support of atomic facts \citep{factscore}. FactsR uses salient facts as an
intermediate representation for healthcare documentation \citep{factsr}. We adopt
the separation of evidence and presentation, then constrain the final fact lines
with templates. TBFact, described with the Healthcare Agent Orchestrator
\citep{hao}, motivates measuring additions and omissions separately. We do not claim
to reproduce its benchmark.

A fact can be located in a document while its interpretation is wrong. NegEx
\citep{negex}, ConText \citep{context} and German negation detection
\citep{cotik} motivate explicit assertion and temporal labels. SummaC
\citep{summac}, AlignScore \citep{alignscore} and MiniCheck \citep{minicheck}
provide approaches to checking consistency between claims and evidence. We retain
location and semantic support as separate checks. We also distinguish strict
contradictions from contextual ambiguity, following the problem formulation studied
by \citet{inconsistency}.

Retrieval-augmented generation combines retrieved evidence with generation
\citep{rag}. Dense passage retrieval \citep{dpr} and lexical ranking address different
matching failures. We combine lexical and vector candidates while reserving absence
claims for a full-record check. For synthetic data, Synthea \citep{synthea},
Asclepius \citep{asclepius} and longitudinal note generation \citep{nhs} motivate
separating patient histories from rendered notes. We require labels to survive in
the final text and distinguish code checks from human annotation.

\section{System Architecture}
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} gives the main data and control paths. We ingest evidence
before serving a lens. The live path selects facts, checks apparent documentation
gaps and validates claims. We store clinician reviews in an append-only overlay.
Accepting a statement records a review action; it does not increase the statement's
support in the documents.

\subsection{Ingestion and Location}
We normalise line breaks and encoded entities while retaining a map to raw offsets.
We segment the normalised text into headings, sentences, list items and medication
rows. The segmenter protects common German abbreviations and joins wrapped prose.
Structured extraction returns a fact kind, an English label, attributes, assertion,
temporal status, event date and a source quote. We locate the quote within its
segment and check sensitive values, including numbers, doses, dates and sides.
We drop failures rather than store an unlocated fact.

We then compare the extracted meaning with the passage and neighbouring context.
A repaired fact must pass location again. A negation trigger can request another
semantic check; it does not decide the clinical assertion. We retain the source date
and document identity on medication lists. We do not describe a historical list as
current medication. LangExtract \citep{langextract} provides a related source-grounded
extraction tool, but its output would still require an independent label check.

For page-based inputs, we retain extracted text together with page and bounding-box
provenance. Text recognition errors can produce apparently valid offsets into an
incorrect transcription. We therefore distinguish text location from agreement with
the rendered page. Image embeddings can retrieve a page; they cannot establish the
support of a clinical statement. End-to-end PDF and scan accuracy remains an
evaluation requirement.

\subsection{Retrieval}
We define knowledge-time eligibility once in the store. For patient $p$, corpus
revision $r$ and as-of date $t$, the eligible document set is
\begin{equation}
 E(p,r,t)=\{d:\operatorname{patient}(d)=p,\ 
 \operatorname{revision}(d)=r,\ \operatorname{docDate}(d)\leq t\}.
\end{equation}
All consumers receive this set. An eligible document can describe a future procedure;
we retain its planned status rather than filtering on the event date. Revision guards
reject reads whose evidence identity changed during the request.

For the small challenge record, the core selector receives every eligible fact of
the relevant kinds. Investigation search and the scale extension use BM25
\citep{bm25} and Gemini embeddings \citep{gemini}, with pgvector storage
\citep{pgvector}. We fuse rank lists $\mathcal R$ with reciprocal rank fusion
\citep{rrf}:
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{RRF}(s)=\sum_{R\in\mathcal R:s\in R}
 \frac{1}{\kappa+\operatorname{rank}_{R}(s)}.
\end{equation}
We restrict both branches to the same patient, revision and eligible documents before
ranking. Duplicate segment identifiers contribute once per branch. A lexical fallback
is recorded when embedding configuration or quota prevents the vector branch from
running. A retrieval hit is a candidate passage, not a supported claim.

\subsection{Lens Selection}
We parse free-text situations into a role, task, concerns and as-of date. Configured
situations use a fixed needs catalog; unfamiliar roles combine catalog entries.
Selection returns eligible fact identifiers, completeness and an optional headline.
It does not return arbitrary card prose. We render the selected facts through
registered templates, run section selection in parallel and stream completed
sections. A section title alone does not count as an answering card.

An empty selection does not prove that information is missing. We expand the concept
with synonyms, check the eligible text lexically and run a full-text model pass over
every eligible document. We show a documentation gap only when these checks succeed
without finding evidence. A lexical failure, full-text failure or timeout produces
an unavailable state. The statement is always relative to the searched record and
as-of date; it is not a statement that the patient lacks the condition.

\subsection{Claim Validation}
Figure~\ref{fig:claims} shows the common claim boundary. Template claims require
valid identifiers, located passages and supported fields. Composed claims additionally
undergo a semantic comparison with the cited facts and source context. Absence claims
require the completed documentation check. We keep clinician annotations and fixed
nonclinical boilerplate separately typed.
\input{figures/claim-flow.tex}

We buffer generated answers until their sentence claims pass. A failed headline
falls back to checked template lines. Letter edits retain citations and trigger
revalidation; unsupported additions remain clinician annotations. Text prepared for
speech uses the validated answer. These paths share evidence identity and validation
results rather than treating each output channel as a new source of truth.

We bind a cached validation to the patient, corpus revision, full evidence window,
validator version, normalised claim text, cited fact identifiers and content hashes,
and segment identifiers. An absence claim can change when an uncited document is
added, which makes revision and window identity necessary even when the claim text
and cited facts are unchanged. Review actions do not alter these machine verdicts.

\subsection{Contradiction Adjudication}
We propose candidates with rules for sides, dates, arithmetic and restated values,
and with a model sweep over contextual evidence. We require evidence that the
statements describe the same event or measurement before classifying a strict
contradiction. Similar wording alone does not establish event identity.

We distinguish contradiction, need for clarification, change over time, measurement
variation, omission and different context. Only contradictions and clarification
requests reach the findings view. Medication changes between dated source lists are
trends. Public titles and explanations pass claim validation, including when a
candidate is downgraded to a clarification. We test expected findings and dismissal
fixtures separately to measure false alarms as well as recovered cases.

\section{Synthetic Data}
We generate structured histories from seeded clinical scenarios, then plan which
parts each document states, negates, paraphrases or omits. We render German documents
from fresh templates and apply controlled mutations such as side flips, dose changes,
date shifts and arithmetic inconsistencies. Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic} shows the
pipeline. We assign patient and generation-family splits before optional rewriting
and hold out styles, scenarios and mutation types.
\input{figures/synthetic-pipeline.tex}

We derive labels from the final rendered documents and the mutation ledger. A
condition present in the hidden history but absent from every eligible document is
not documented in the record. It must not become a supported claim merely because
we know the generated history. We check that intended values, spans and mutations
survive a prose rewrite; if a check fails, we retain the original rendering.

The saved manifest reports \SynthPatients{} generated patients,
\SynthDocuments{} documents and \SynthFacts{} code-checked facts. It records
\SynthContradictions{} contradictions, \SynthDistractors{} distractors and
\SynthGaps{} documentation gaps. These counts describe generated artifacts, not
clinical prevalence, an ingested database cohort or independently verified semantic
labels. Human approval of an aggregate count also does not make the underlying
labels human gold. We follow the documentation goals of dataset datasheets
\citep{datasheets} and keep the annotation source and label tier explicit.

Bulk data, rendered pages and model artifacts remain local. Only a fresh synthetic
sample is eligible for repository distribution. We do not use challenge prose in
templates, training examples, publication examples or source-controlled test fixtures.
A separate human-reviewed sample is needed to estimate label noise and clinical
plausibility.

\section{Verifier Training}
We formulate verification as classification of a claim and a passage with context
into supported, unsupported or contradicted. We construct hard negatives from
controlled mutations and keep assertion and timing information. We split by patient
and family before adaptation. The training, validation and test manifests contain
\TrainExamples{}, \ValidationExamples{} and \TestExamples{} examples, respectively.
We keep real-patient weak labels outside training and report them separately.

We adapt a quantised Qwen3 base \citep{qwen} with low-rank updates \citep{lora} using
MLX on Apple silicon \citep{mlx}. For a frozen weight matrix $W_0$, the adapted
projection has the form
\begin{equation}
 h=W_0x+\frac{\alpha}{r}BAx,
 \qquad A\in\mathbb R^{r\times d},\quad B\in\mathbb R^{m\times r}.
\end{equation}
The quantised-base approach is related to QLoRA \citep{qlora}. We do not equate this
MLX run with the original CUDA implementation or assert that it uses all of its
quantisation methods. We mask the prompt during training and bound the input length
at \SequenceLimit{} tokens. The configured wall-time cap is \TrainingMinuteCap{}
minutes. A cap and an epoch target do not establish that a run completed.

We compare rules-only checks, the configured proxy semantic judge and the local
adapter. We measure macro F-score, coverage and latency together. We reserve model
confidence and calibration for experiments with recorded probabilities; a class
prediction alone does not define a reliability curve. Calibration analysis follows
the distinction between confidence and accuracy made by \citet{guo}. We use a model
card to record the training conditions and intended use \citep{modelcards}.
The proxy judge remains the default semantic backend unless a separate integration
decision approves the local verifier.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
We keep the challenge regression, blind challenge situations, synthetic pipeline,
retrieval and verifier experiments separate. Evaluation uses an empty review layer.
The regression protocol specifies \RegressionTarget{} answers across
\SituationCount{} configured situations. The blind set specifies \HeldoutTarget{}
independently authored situations. The paper build does not open its results by
default; release requires the orchestrator's scheduled review.

All empirical quantities enter through \texttt{generated/numbers.tex}. We extract
only numeric aggregates from allowlisted artifact fields and record input hashes.
A human review must approve a value against the complete current source identity
before the manuscript prints it. Missing, invalid and unapproved values render as
\textit{pending}; they never become zero observations. The machine-readable aggregate
file remains available for that review. No approval of measurements is implied by
this manuscript build.

\subsection{Limitations}
We have a prototype, a narrow challenge workflow and synthetic data whose labels
primarily follow code checks. We have not established clinical efficacy, diagnostic
accuracy, reduced clinician workload or safety in deployment. Template rendering
limits unsupported composition but cannot repair an incorrect extraction. A model
judge can share systematic errors with the extractor. A source citation can be
correctly located while the statement loses a qualification in translation.

Synthetic held-out families test a bounded kind of variation. They do not substitute
for external records from different institutions, independent clinicians or new
writing conventions. Real-patient weak labels measure agreement with a validator;
they do not provide an adjudicated clinical test set. We also need independent
page-level verification for scans and PDFs. We do not infer a speed and quality
tradeoff from runs with different populations or measurement conditions.

The record contains sensitive clinical content even when the challenge describes
it as synthetic. We retain access restrictions and omit document text from this
report. Before clinical use, we would need independent annotation, error analysis
by claim type, prospective workflow evaluation and access-control review. A
clinician review action is an authorship record, not a substitute for these studies.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion}
We presented a task-specific clinical view built from located evidence. We separate
fact selection from rendering, apply one validation boundary across output channels
and preserve the distinction between missing documentation and a failed check.
We described a synthetic data pipeline and a local verifier experiment with separate
evaluation tracks. The next empirical step is independent annotation and matched
comparisons of coverage, support and latency. Until then, we treat this system as an
inspectable prototype with explicit unresolved measurements.
